codæ

Contract-driven agentic engineering.

Coding is going dark factory. Specs in, production system out, no humans on the factory floor. The multi-year trajectory of agentic engineering points there — and the industry-promised 10–100× speedup lives nowhere else. The obstacle on the way is AI slop and semantic drift, measured in 2026 across real codebases. This whitepaper argues that the bridge is a contract: a set of mutually-derived specs, tied together by a graph, simple enough for humans to mend and rich enough to keep agents from drifting.

§1 — The destinationCoding goes dark factory.

A "dark factory" in manufacturing runs lights-out — no humans on the floor, just machines. The product flows in as a specification and out as physical units. Workers exist; they design the line, audit outputs, evolve the spec. They do not turn the wrenches.

The same pattern is forming in software. The highest-leverage end-state of agentic coding is the dark factory: agents write the code, run the tests, fix the failures, open the pull requests, and merge them. Humans set direction, govern the contract that steers the factory, and audit the artifacts that flow out. The factory floor is dark.

The pattern is no longer aspirational. In February 2026, Spotify's co-CEO confirmed that the company's best engineers had not written a line of code since December — instead, they were merging hundreds of agent-authored pull requests per month through Spotify's internal coding-agent platform.17 BCG Platinion's Dark Software Factory report, also early 2026, named the milestone explicitly: "as few as three engineers can run a software factory where humans no longer write code."18 Sequoia, in their January 2026 essay declaring the year of AGI, framed it as a capability threshold crossed: coding agents that plan, use tools, maintain state, and loop until success.19

"Agentic engineering is about preserving the quality bar of what existed before in professional software. You're still responsible for your software just as before, but can you go faster? You have these agents which are spiky, fallible, a little stochastic, but extremely powerful. How do you coordinate them to go faster without sacrificing your quality bar?"

— Andrej Karpathy, "From Vibe Coding to Agentic Engineering," Sequoia AI Ascent 202520

The economic argument writes itself. The industry-promised 10× to 100× productivity gain from agentic coding lives in exactly one place: the dark factory. The moment the human has to drop into the code to fix what the agent built — even for a small fraction of changes — the speedup collapses. Reading 100,000 lines to safely change 100 isn't a 100× operation. It's barely 1×. The promise breaks the moment the human is required.

The dark factory does not eliminate engineers; it relocates them. As Karpathy put it: "You have to be in charge of the aesthetics, the judgment, the taste, and the oversight. People have to be in charge of this spec, this plan."20 The role moves up the stack — from coder to architect, from typing to governing.

codæ commits to dark-factory coding as the multi-year trajectory, not as a v0.3 promise. Today's agents aren't trustworthy enough to leave entirely alone with the code. The arc is real, the destination is realistic, the work to get there is hard. The rest of this whitepaper is about what's standing in the way and how to bridge it.

§2 — The obstacleAI slop is what stands between us and the dark factory.

Dark-factory coding is plausible because agents have crossed the capability threshold. It hasn't shipped at scale because of one thing: AI slop — code that looks reasonable, passes review-by-glance, and accumulates faster than reviewers can absorb. Drift that is no longer an aberration; in 2026 it is the equilibrium of long-running agentic generation.

The same failure shows up under three names in the 2026 literature:

  • Semantic drift — outputs progressively diverge from original task intent while remaining syntactically valid. Now widely named in 2026 reporting on long-running AI coding.
  • AI slop — cheap to generate, expensive to review. A 1,154-post analysis of developer-forum discussions coded fifteen recurring themes, all converging on this pattern.1
  • Comprehension debt — the codebase looks clean, but no human (and no incoming agent) understands what it's doing or why. Coined formally this year in a study of 207 students across 8 weeks of agentic-Scrum projects.2

The literature has moved from anecdotal to instrumented over the last twelve months. References below are primarily 2026, grounded in peer-reviewed venues, official industry reports, or large-N empirical studies. Many are arXiv preprints; treat them accordingly. The pattern is consistent.

The most precise empirical study this year is SlopCodeBench3: 20 problems × 93 checkpoints × 11 models, instrumented to measure how agent code degrades when agents iteratively extend their own prior code under evolving specs. The finding that deserves to be famous: no agent solves a whole problem end-to-end. Agent code runs 2.2× more verbose than comparable open-source Python repos.

The slop pattern is also visible in the wild. Liu et al.13 tracked 302.6k verified AI-authored commits across 6,299 GitHub repositories and identified 484,366 issues introduced; 22.7% of those tracked AI-introduced issues survive at latest revision. The social side has been characterized too: Baltes, Cheong, and Treude1 coded 1,154 developer-forum posts and frame the result as a software-commons problem14 — generation gains for one developer become review and maintenance costs for everyone else.

The mechanism behind the deterioration is well documented. Chroma's "Context Rot" report4 (a July 2025 technical report, included here because nothing comparable has been published in 2026) tested 18 frontier models including GPT-4.1, Claude 4, Gemini 2.5, and Qwen3: every single one becomes unreliable as input length grows, even on controlled tasks. SWE Context Bench11 adds the corollary — context quality, not quantity, is what improves agent accuracy and cost.

"AI amplifies the quality of the engineering system it operates within."

— DORA 2025 report on AI-assisted development6

Organizations with mature processes get the upside. Organizations with fragmented systems get accelerated drift. The data does not support a neutral case.

Across all of the above, the same shape appears: agentic generation works as a bounded operation but degrades when bound to long-running, multi-component, multi-agent systems where coherence has to be preserved. The bottleneck is not model capability. The bottleneck is the absence of a durable, inspectable, traceable control surface above the code — and the dark factory cannot ship without one.

Slop is not a small obstacle. It is the obstacle. The next three sections explain why every existing tactical fix — better prompts, more context, sharper instructions — runs out of road before it gets us to dark factory.

§3 — Why prompts don't scaleThe first instinct is local. It runs out of road.

If slop is the obstacle, the first instinct is to fight it locally — better prompts, sharper instructions, more elaborate steering files, stricter rules. This works for one run. It dies on the next agent, the next session, the next contributor — and never gets us to dark factory.

Chat history isn't memory. The next agent doesn't read what you told the last one. Every cycle starts cold; the codebase is the only durable artifact — and by this point, it's too large for any agent or human to internalize from scratch.

The cumulative cost is now measurable. An Anthropic study cited in O'Reilly's reporting7 found that developers using AI assistance scored 17% lower on comprehension quizzes after completing equivalent tasks, with the largest decline in debugging. The codebase looks clean. The reckoning arrives quietly.

"Making code cheap to generate doesn't make understanding cheap to skip. The comprehension work is the job."

— Addy Osmani, O'Reilly Radar, Apr 20267

Prompts are a tactical fix for a structural problem. The structural fix lives somewhere more durable than the chat window.

§4 — The cognitive surfaceIf humans go upstairs, what can they hold?

The dark factory only works if humans actually stay above the code. So the load-bearing question is: what surface CAN humans hold? The answer isn't lines or tokens — it's the number of meaningful items in the affected review cut. Contract-level: IDed semantic items — capabilities, constraints, requirements, flows, stories, acceptance criteria, bounded contexts, views, interactions, components, interfaces, behaviors, decisions. Code-level: files, classes/types, methods/functions, endpoints/handlers, branches, tests, integrations, runtime resources, dependency edges.

A codebase grows roughly as features × accumulation: every new branch, edge case, retry path, glue, integration adds items. Comprehension doesn't scale linearly with that growth. The working-memory ceiling is hard — humans plateau around 7±2 elements; cognitive-load research formalizes the rest. Above that ceiling, code becomes a foreign object: readable line by line, opaque as a system.

A contract is a different shape. It captures what the system is, not how every line implements it. A small set of concepts at the right level of abstraction can cover a large set of code paths. The contract is, by construction, a smaller item surface than the code it governs — small enough for a human to fit in their head, rich enough to keep agents from drifting.

Until dogfood data lands, the modest claim: humans review tens of contract items, not hundreds or thousands of code items. Report the ratio; don't flourish it.

Cognitive surface, to scale. A 108K-line system (left) lies far above any human comprehension ceiling. The contract governing it (right) — intent, requirements, structure, behavior, config — is roughly 24% of the code volume and fits below the ceiling. Humans hold the right side. Agents extend the left. The contract is the bridge.8

One empirical anchor (using lines as a coarse proxy for items): Vasilopoulos's 2026 field study8 reports a 108,000-line C# distributed system governed by ~660 lines of hot-memory constitution, 19 specialized agents (~9,300 lines), and 34 on-demand specification documents (~16,250 lines), across 283 development sessions. Even by the cruder line-based proxy, the contract is a fraction of the code and is sufficient to keep agents pattern-adherent at scale. The illustration above shows the rough proportion. Item-count ratios will be reported once VibeLoom dogfood data exists.

Cognitive-load-aware refactoring research9 confirms the principle from a different angle. Across 974 MBPP programs, 5,000 APPS programs, and a human study of 20 programmers: structures aligned to working-memory limits reduce failures by 54–71% versus unconstrained prompting and improve comprehension scores by 17–31% across multiple measures. Structure that fits the cognitive surface is not a stylistic preference. It's an empirical performance lever.

"Treating project documentation as infrastructure rather than artifact: living specifications that AI agents depend on to produce correct, convention-adherent code."

— Vasilopoulos, Codified Context, Feb 20268

§5 — Memory and evalThe contract is what survives the next agent.

A contract at the right cognitive surface is necessary but not sufficient. The deeper claim is that the same contract, once it exists, does two more things at once: it gives the next agent long-term memory of what the system is, and it gives the generation step an eval signal that what it just produced can be checked against. Both are prerequisites for the dark factory.

The contract as long-term memory

When chat is cheap and context is rotting, the only persistent representation of "what this system is supposed to be" is the contract. Anthropic's 2026 Agentic Coding Trends Report10 puts it bluntly: engineers use AI in roughly 60% of work but can fully delegate only 0–20%. The constraint isn't model capability. It's context.

Anthropic's own Claude Code best-practices guidance makes the same point operationally: "Claude doesn't really have memory. The main way we share state across sessions or across our team is this CLAUDE.md file."21 A single file is the entry-level case; a contract is the structured generalization — durable across sessions, inspectable, mendable.

"The gap isn't capability. The gap is that fully delegating a task requires the agent to have the right context to complete it."

— Anthropic, 2026 Agentic Coding Trends Report10

The empirical receipt comes from SWE Context Bench11: 1,100 base tasks across 51 repos and 9 languages. Correctly selected, summarized context — i.e., distilled intent and structure — improves resolution accuracy and reduces runtime/token cost. Unfiltered or wrongly selected context provides negative benefit. Quality of context, not quantity. A contract is the curated, summarized, high-signal context the next agent needs.

The contract as eval signal

The same artifacts work as the eval surface. When the agent generates downstream content — more specs, code, tests — everything it produces can be checked against the approved upstream, both structurally (does the new artifact derive from the right inputs?) and semantically (does it faithfully represent the upstream meaning?).

REA-Coder12 tested this directly: align requirements before generation, verify after, iterate on misalignments. Across 4 LLMs and 5 benchmarks, the result was 7.93% to 30.25% Pass@1 improvement. The binding constraint on code-generation quality isn't model capability — it's requirement understanding. Fix the spec; the code follows.

That is not a proof that contract-driven agentic engineering eliminates drift end-to-end. It is supporting evidence that requirement-alignment-before-generation scales as a real mechanism — not an aspiration — when paired with verification. The pattern is the right shape to lean on, and the right shape to scale toward dark factory.

§6 — The bridgeThe contract is what gets us across.

"The 10–100× speedup is real only when the human stays out of the code. The contract is what keeps them out."

— codæ thesis

The choice underneath the manifesto is binary, and worth saying out loud.

One option: keep code human-mendable. The agent drafts; the human reviews, edits, ships. We lived in this world for two years — and it's the world the industry-promised 10× to 100× productivity lives in. It also has an ugly back-of-the-envelope: the moment the human has to drop into the code to fix what the agent built — even for a small fraction of changes — the speedup collapses. Code's cognitive surface (§4) is what it is. Reading 100,000 lines to safely change 100 isn't a 100× operation. It's barely 1×. The promise breaks the moment the human is required. This path leads away from dark factory, not toward it.

The other option — the one codæ builds the bridge to: coding becomes the dark factory. Code is generated, regenerated, never maintained by hand. The human's mendable surface moves up the stack — from the code to the contract above it. The contract is what the human comprehends, governs, edits. The code is what the agent compiles down to.

For the bridge to hold, the contract has to do four jobs at once:

  • Edited by humans — so it must be smaller than the code (§4).
  • Kept consistent and coherent with AI's help — so it must be machine-checkable.
  • The long-term memory the next agent inherits — durable across sessions, independent of chat (§5).
  • The eval signal every generated artifact is verified against — drift becomes detectable, not hopeful.

A set of mutually-derived complementary specs, tied together by a graph that makes the relationships explicit and machine-checkable — that's a contract. It satisfies all four jobs. That's the codæ proposition. The 10–100× speedup survives only if the human never touches the factory floor. Keep them upstairs. Give them a contract.

§7 — The codæ thesisContract-driven agentic engineering.

The threads pull together cleanly:

  • The problem is structural — drift compounds at machine speed under agentic generation.
  • The mechanism is mechanical — chat is not memory, context rots, agents re-derive their understanding every turn.
  • The surface humans can hold is the contract — measured by the number of IDed semantic items in the affected review cut, not by lines of code.
  • Contract-style enforcement on agents is now empirically supported (§5) — not yet proven to bound drift universally, but real enough to lean on.

From these, the codæ thesis follows directly: govern agentic code generation by a contract — a set of mutually-derived specs tied together by a graph — so that humans can leave the factory floor. The contract is smaller than the code, persists across agents, is verifiable against the code, and is approved by humans. (The methodology spells out how each property is implemented at the artifact level — see vibeloom-methodology.md. This whitepaper stops at the paradigm.)

An hommage to Design by Contract

codæ is an hommage to Bertrand Meyer's Design by Contract16, not an equivalence. Meyer's discipline made obligations explicit between software elements through preconditions, postconditions, and invariants — and crucially made those obligations decidable: boolean-checkable at runtime, sound for the property they encoded. codæ inherits the discipline of explicit obligations and the principle that downstream artifacts must satisfy upstream contracts. It does not yet inherit decidability — today's contract checks are semantic (heuristic eval), not boolean.

The trajectory is concrete: VibeLoom organizes eval on a verification ladder of decidable structural checks, mechanical validation runners, and heuristic semantic eval. v0.3 ships an inventory across all three tiers. Each release should promote checks upward — heuristic dimensions become mechanical; mechanical become structural. The decidable share grows as the engine matures. See methodology §14.3 for the ladder and the v0.3 inventory.

The destination is grandiose on purpose: agentic engineering may make it realistic to resurface the long-standing dream of formally verifiable software, this time at the contract scope rather than the routine scope. That destination is years away. The ladder is the climb.

From spec-driven to contract-driven

There is a name for the immediate predecessor: spec-driven development, the movement now naming itself across tools like AWS Kiro, GitHub Spec Kit, and BMAD. The 2026 SDD taxonomy15 places existing approaches across three rigor levels — spec-first (specs guide, then drift), spec-anchored (specs and code co-evolve with enforcement), spec-as-source (specs alone are edited; code is regenerated). All three are real progress over prompt-only generation. codæ is the next level: contract-driven — not one spec for one file or one feature, but the multi-tier, mutually-derived, graph-tied, drift-detecting contract that governs the whole lifecycle. Same impulse, larger surface, longer arc.

The compiler was software's first dark factory

We used to inspect generated machine code. Now we inspect source, trust the toolchain, test the result, and debug through traces. codæ proposes a similar move one abstraction level up: humans inspect the contract; agents compile it into implementation. In VibeLoom, the artifact parser is the frontend, the contract graph is the intermediate representation, traces are source maps, and generated code targets a platform contract such as AWS, GCP, Cloudflare, or a local runtime. This is an analogy, not a proof — a Rust compiler is deterministic, agentic generation is not — but it clarifies the desired control surface and the toolchain that will earn it.

This is what we mean by contract-driven agentic engineering: the contract is what humans hold while agents compile it into a production system. The factory floor goes dark; the contract stays mendable; the destination is real. We call the discipline codæ.
